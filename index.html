The Birthday Paradox

One day in office hours I asked my Probability Theory prof Sky Cao to tell me more about the (birthday) paradox, which came up when I was reading more on finite fields, hoping to gain a sense of what might have already been done for dynamics on finite fields (specifically, Pollard's rho algorithm for discrete logarithms.) I really liked his explanation. With his permission, I've written an informal version down here.

  • Suppose we have \(N\) balls and at each time step, randomly pick a ball with replacement.
  • We denote the random variable \(T\) to return the timestep that is the first time a ball is picked twice.
  • We are interested in the number of people that must be in a room for us to say there are “probably” 2 people who share the same birthday.
  • The chance of 2 people sharing the same birthday is analogous to the chance of picking the same ball twice, with \(N = 365\).
  • It turns out that \(T \sim N^{1/2}\), meaning the size of \(T\) is approximately \(N^{1/2}\).
  • To prove \(T \sim N^{1/2}\), we must find an expression for \(1 - \mathbb{P}(T \leq k) = \mathbb{P}(T > k)\) in terms of \(N\) and \(k\), where \(k\) is some unknown function of \(N\).

To verify that we can set \(k \sim N^{1/2}\), it suffices to check:

(1) when \(k \gg N^{1/2}\), we have \(\mathbb{P}(T > k) \sim 0\)
and
(2) when \(k \ll N^{1/2}\), we have \(\mathbb{P}(T > k) \sim 1\)

Then

\[ \mathbb{P}(T > k) \;\overset{(1)}{=}\; \frac{N \cdot (N-1) \cdots (N-(k-1))}{\underbrace{N \cdots N}_{k \text{ times}}} \]

(1) \(\mathbb{P}(T > k)\) is the probability that we needed more than \(k\) times to get a repeat. This is the same as the probability that in the first \(k\) times, we did not get a repeat.

\[ = \left(\frac{N}{N}\right)\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right) \cdots \left(\frac{N-(k-1)}{N}\right) \] \[ = 1\left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right) \cdots \left(1 - \frac{k-1}{N}\right) \] \[ \overset{(2)}{\approx}\; \exp(0)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N}\right) \cdots \exp\!\left(\frac{-k+1}{N}\right) \]

(2) Observe that for \(|x| \ll 1\), we have the first order approximation \(e^x \approx 1 + x\) from the Taylor series of \(e^x\).

\[ = \exp\!\left(-\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} j\right)\right) \] \[ = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}k(k-1)\right)\right) \]

But now we see

\(\dfrac{1}{e^{k^2/N}} \to 0\) as \(e^{k^2/N} \to \infty\),
that is, as \(k \gg N^{1/2}\), as claimed

\(\dfrac{1}{e^{k^2/N}} \to 1\) as \(e^{k^2/N} \to 1\),
that is, as \(k \ll N^{1/2}\) as claimed

[issue: we stated \(1 \leq k \leq N\), this only works if \(k \to 0\), but \(1 \leq k\)?]
